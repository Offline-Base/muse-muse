# Opinion Writing Rubric, Grade 4

Prompt: "Should our school have a garden? Explain your opinion."

Score each criterion from 1 to 4. The total is out of 16. A 3 means the essay meets the grade 4 standard; a 4 goes beyond it. Score what is on the page, not what the student meant to say. Spelling is scored only under Conventions.

## Ideas

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | States a clear opinion in the first lines and keeps it all the way through. Every reason connects back to the opinion, and the writer thinks about the other side (for example, "Some people might say...") and answers it. |
| 3 | States a clear opinion and gives two or three reasons that support it. The essay stays on topic, though one reason may be thin or loosely connected. |
| 2 | The opinion is there but may be buried, unclear or changing. Reasons are listed but not explained, or the essay drifts into a story that does not support the opinion. |
| 1 | No clear opinion, or the essay is mostly off topic. Ideas are a list of things the writer likes without saying why they matter. |

## Organization

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | Has an introduction that hooks the reader, body paragraphs with one reason each, and a conclusion that sums up. Uses a variety of transitions (First, In addition, However, In conclusion) that make the essay easy to follow. |
| 3 | Has a beginning, a middle and an end. Reasons are grouped into paragraphs and joined with simple transitions (First, Second, Also). |
| 2 | Some grouping of ideas, but the order jumps around or paragraphs mix several reasons. The ending is missing or just stops. Few or no transitions. |
| 1 | Little or no structure. Ideas are in one block or a string of run-on sentences, and the reader has to work to follow them. |

## Evidence and details

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | Each reason is backed by specific, well-chosen details: facts, examples from class or life, or numbers. The writer explains how each detail proves the point. |
| 3 | Most reasons have at least one relevant detail or example. Some explanation of how the detail supports the reason. |
| 2 | Details are general ("it is good," "it is fun") or only loosely related. Personal stories are told but not tied back to the opinion. |
| 1 | Few or no details. Reasons are stated with nothing to back them up. |

## Conventions

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| 4 | Capitals, end marks and commas are nearly always correct. Grade-level words are spelled correctly, and sentences vary in length and structure. Errors are rare and do not distract. |
| 3 | Mostly correct capitals and end marks. A few spelling errors on harder words, but common words are spelled right. Errors do not get in the way of meaning. |
| 2 | Frequent errors in capitals, punctuation or spelling of common words (becuase, are for our). Some run-on sentences. The reader can still understand the essay. |
| 1 | Errors on most lines make the essay hard to read. Missing capitals and end marks, many misspelled common words, long run-ons. |

## Feedback for each essay

For every essay, write down:

- the score for each criterion and the total out of 16,
- one strength the student should keep doing,
- one next step, small enough to work on in the next essay,
- a short feedback paragraph written to the student, warm and specific.

Keep feedback about the writing. Do not mention anything personal the student shared (family, health, home) in feedback that leaves the classroom.
